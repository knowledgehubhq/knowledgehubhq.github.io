How to Spot a Phishing Email or Text: 12 Warning Signs

By the Everyday Tech Help editorial team · Published September 27, 2026

Phishing is the most common way ordinary people get scammed online — not through sophisticated hacking, but through a convincing email or text message that tricks you into handing over your password, bank details, or personal information. The messages keep getting more polished, but they almost always contain telltale signs. Learn these twelve warning signs and you'll catch the vast majority of them before they catch you.

What phishing actually looks like

Forget the image of a badly written email from a foreign prince. Modern phishing usually impersonates someone you trust: your bank, a delivery company, your phone carrier, a streaming service, or even your boss. The message creates urgency — "your account will be locked," "a package couldn't be delivered," "unusual sign-in detected" — and pushes you to click a link or call a number right now. Urgency is the scammer's best weapon, because it stops you from thinking.

12 warning signs

1. A manufactured sense of urgency

"Act within 24 hours or your account will be suspended." Legitimate companies do send security alerts, but they rarely threaten immediate consequences to force a fast click. Urgency is designed to bypass your judgment.

2. Unexpected attachments

An invoice, receipt, or "secure document" you weren't expecting. Attachments can carry malware — never open one from an unexpected message, even if the sender name looks familiar.

3. The sender address doesn't match

The display name says "Your Bank" but the actual email address is something like support@secure-banking-alerts.net. Always expand or hover over the sender name to see the real address. For texts, check whether the number is an ordinary mobile number rather than an official short code.

4. Generic greetings

"Dear Customer" or "Dear Account Holder" instead of your name. Companies you do business with know your name. (Though note: some scammers now use names from data breaches, so a personal greeting alone doesn't prove legitimacy.)

5. Links that don't go where they claim

On a computer, hover over a link without clicking — your browser shows the real destination in the corner. On a phone, press and hold the link to preview it. If the text says "yourbank.com" but the real address is a jumble of random characters or a lookalike domain, it's phishing.

6. Requests for passwords, PINs, or full card numbers

No legitimate bank, government agency, or well-run company will ever ask for your full password, PIN, or complete card number by email or text. Ever. Any message that does is a scam, full stop.

7. Too-good-to-be-true offers

You've won a prize you never entered, been selected for a refund you didn't request, or offered a job paying absurd money for no work. If you didn't enter, apply, or buy — it's bait.

8. Threats and intimidation

Fake tax authority demands, "your computer is infected" pop-ups, or messages claiming to have compromising footage of you. These rely on panic. Real authorities don't collect debts or issue warnings via threatening text messages.

9. Slightly-off company names and logos

Look closely: "Micros0ft" with a zero, an extra letter in a brand name, or a logo that's just slightly blurry or wrong. Scammers count on you glancing, not examining.

10. Poor grammar — but don't rely on this anymore

Bad spelling used to be the classic giveaway, and it still appears. But with modern tools, many phishing messages are now perfectly written. Treat clean grammar as meaningless — its absence is a clue, its presence proves nothing.

11. The message arrived out of the blue about an account you don't have

A security alert from a bank you've never used, or a delivery notice when you ordered nothing. Scammers send millions of these hoping a fraction match real customers.

12. You're asked to "verify" by clicking rather than going direct

The message insists you use its link. A legitimate company is always fine with you opening your browser and typing their address yourself, or opening their official app. Scammers need you to use their link because that's where the trap is.

What to do when you get a suspicious message

  1. Don't click, don't reply, don't call any number in the message. Replying confirms your number is active.
  2. Verify independently. If it's supposedly your bank, open your banking app or type the bank's address into your browser yourself. If it's a delivery, open the courier's official site and enter your tracking number there.
  3. Report it. Most email apps have a "Report phishing" option. On phones, you can forward scam texts to your carrier's spam reporting number (in many countries this is 7726, which spells SPAM).
  4. Delete it. Once reported, delete the message so nobody else in your household acts on it later.

If you already clicked or entered information

Don't panic — act quickly:

  1. Change the password on the affected account immediately, from a device you trust, by going directly to the real site (not through any link).
  2. Turn on two-factor authentication for that account if you haven't already.
  3. If you entered bank or card details, call your bank right away using the number on the back of your card. They can watch for fraud and issue a new card.
  4. Check your accounts over the next few weeks for anything you don't recognize.
  5. Run a security scan if you downloaded an attachment — use your phone or computer's built-in security tools.

Frequently asked questions

Can scammers fake the sender so it looks like it's really from my bank?

Yes — email sender names are easy to fake, and scammers can even make a text appear in the same conversation thread as genuine messages from a company. That's why you should verify through the official app or website, never through links in the message.

Are package delivery texts always scams?

Not always — but unsolicited ones usually are. If you're genuinely expecting a parcel, check the tracking on the courier's official website rather than clicking the text's link.

What about phone calls from "my bank's fraud department"?

Treat unexpected calls the same way. Hang up and call back on the official number from your card or the company's website. Caller ID can be faked.

I'm embarrassed I fell for one. Should I tell anyone?

Yes — tell your bank if money or account details were involved, and consider telling family members so they learn the warning signs too. These scams are designed by professionals to fool busy people; there's no shame in it, and quick action limits the damage.
