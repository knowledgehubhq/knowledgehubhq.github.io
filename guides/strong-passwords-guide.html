How to Create Strong Passwords You'll Actually Remember

By the Everyday Tech Help editorial team · Published September 27, 2026

Most password advice fails for a simple reason: it asks humans to behave like computers. "Use 16 random characters, never reuse them, change them monthly" is technically good advice that nobody can follow — so people reuse one password everywhere, and one breach exposes everything. There's a better way. This guide shows you how to create passwords that are both genuinely strong and possible for a normal person to live with.

Why password reuse is the real danger

Here's the threat in plain terms: when a website gets hacked, criminals collect the email-and-password pairs and try them on other sites — email, banking, shopping. This is called credential stuffing, and it's automated. If you use the same password on ten sites and one of them leaks, all ten accounts are effectively compromised. The strength of your password matters less than its uniqueness. A different password on every important account is the single biggest upgrade you can make.

The passphrase method: strong passwords you can remember

Forget random strings like "Tr7#kP9!z." Instead, build a passphrase: four or five random words strung together. For example:

correct horse battery staple

This famous example illustrates the principle: four ordinary words are far easier to remember than a jumble of characters, yet enormously harder for a computer to guess — because the number of possible combinations is astronomically larger. To make your own:

  1. Pick 4–5 random words that have nothing to do with each other. Open a book to random pages, look around the room, use whatever comes to mind. The key word is random — not a phrase, not song lyrics, not "ilovemydog2024."
  2. Add one twist: capitalize one word, add a number or symbol somewhere. Example: correct-Horse-battery-7-staple.
  3. Make each important account's passphrase different. You don't need unique passphrases for everything (see password managers below), but your email, bank, and main accounts each deserve their own.

Your email password deserves special attention: if someone controls your email, they can reset passwords everywhere else. Make your email passphrase the strongest one you own.

Password managers: the real solution

A passphrase gets you partway there, but nobody can remember 80 unique passphrases. That's what a password manager is for — an app that generates, stores, and fills in strong random passwords for every site, so you only need to remember one master passphrase.

Start with what's already on your phone

You may not need to install anything. Both major platforms include a solid built-in manager:

  • iPhone: Go to Settings → Passwords. Your iPhone can generate strong passwords when you sign up for sites, store them securely, and fill them in with Face ID. They sync across your Apple devices via iCloud Keychain.
  • Android: Google's password manager lives in Settings → Google → Autofill → Google Password Manager (or in Chrome under Settings → Password Manager). It offers to save and generate passwords, and fills them in with your fingerprint.

Using it day to day

  1. When you create a new account, accept the suggested strong password instead of typing your own.
  2. Let the manager save it. From then on, your fingerprint or face unlock fills it in.
  3. For existing accounts with reused passwords, change the important ones (email, bank, shopping) to generated passwords one at a time — ten minutes a week and you'll be done in a month.

Add two-factor authentication (2FA)

Two-factor authentication means logging in requires your password plus a second proof — usually a code from an app or a text message. Even if someone steals your password, they can't get in without that second factor. Turn it on for at minimum:

  • Your email account
  • Your bank and financial accounts
  • Social media accounts
  • Anything holding payment details

You'll find it under "Security" or "Sign-in" settings, usually labeled "2-Step Verification" or "Two-Factor Authentication." An authenticator app (like Google Authenticator or your phone's built-in option) is more secure than text-message codes, but text-message codes are far better than nothing.

What about writing passwords down?

Security professionals have quietly softened on this: a password written on paper in your desk drawer is far safer than reusing "Fluffy123" on forty websites. Online criminals can't reach your drawer. If writing them down is what it takes to use unique passwords, do it — just keep the paper somewhere private, not stuck to your monitor.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I change my passwords?

The old "change every 90 days" advice is outdated. Forced frequent changes just make people pick weaker passwords. Change a password when there's a reason: a breach notification, suspicious activity, or you shared it with someone who no longer needs it.

Is it safe to let my browser save passwords?

Reasonably, if your device itself is secured with a PIN, fingerprint, or face unlock. The built-in managers on iPhone and Android encrypt the stored passwords. It's vastly safer than reusing passwords. Just make sure your phone's lock screen is actually enabled.

What if I forget my password manager's master password?

This is the one password you truly cannot lose — there's no "forgot password" for the vault itself. Write your master passphrase on paper and store it somewhere safe at home when you first set it up. Some managers also let you set up account recovery options; enable them.

Are password manager apps from the app store safe?

The well-known ones use strong encryption and have been independently audited. Stick to reputable names, download only from the official app store, and enable the app's own biometric lock. Avoid obscure "free password vault" apps with few reviews.
