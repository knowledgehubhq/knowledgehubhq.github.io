Video Calls for Beginners: A Setup Guide That Works

By the Everyday Tech Help editorial team · Published September 27, 2026

Whether it's a doctor's appointment, a job interview, a grandchild's birthday, or a work meeting, video calls are now part of everyday life. If you've been avoiding them because the technology feels intimidating, this guide is for you. We'll go slowly: picking an app, making your first call, looking and sounding decent, and fixing the problems that come up most often. No prior experience assumed.

Step 1: Pick one app and stick with it

There are many video call apps, and they all do roughly the same thing. The best one is whichever the person you're calling already uses. Here's a quick overview:

AppBest forWhat you need to know
WhatsAppCalling family and friendsMost people already have it; calls are free over Wi-Fi
FaceTimeiPhone/iPad/Mac users calling each otherBuilt into Apple devices — no setup needed
Google MeetAppointments and scheduled meetingsFree; you join from a link in your browser or the app
ZoomWork meetings, classes, group callsFree for shorter meetings; you usually just click a link

Tip: ask the other person "which app should I use?" before the call. Installing the app they suggest saves everyone trouble.

Step 2: Set up before the call (not during)

Do this at least 15 minutes before your first call, when there's no pressure:

  1. Install the app from your phone's app store (or open the website on a computer).
  2. Grant the permissions it asks for — camera and microphone access. Without these, the other person can't see or hear you. If you accidentally tapped "Don't Allow," you can fix it later in your phone's Settings under the app's name.
  3. Sign in or create an account if the app requires it. Some (like Zoom or Google Meet) let you join a call as a guest with just a link — no account needed.
  4. Do a test call. Most apps let you preview your camera before joining. Zoom has a test meeting feature; in WhatsApp, you can start a call with a willing friend or family member first. Check: can you see yourself? Is the microphone picking up your voice?
  5. Charge your device or plug it in. Video calls drain batteries fast.

Step 3: Joining a call from a link

Most scheduled calls (doctor, work, classes) arrive as a link in an email or message. Here's the universal process:

  1. Tap or click the link a few minutes early.
  2. Your browser or the app will open. If prompted, tap Join or Join Meeting.
  3. You may wait in a "waiting room" until the host lets you in — this is normal.
  4. When asked, allow camera and microphone access, then make sure you're unmuted (look for a microphone icon without a line through it).

If the link asks for a meeting ID or passcode, they'll be in the same message as the link — type them in when prompted.

Look and sound better with three easy changes

You don't need special equipment. These three things make the biggest difference:

1. Light your face, not your back

Sit facing a window or lamp. If the bright light is behind you, you'll appear as a dark silhouette. This one change improves your video more than any camera upgrade.

2. Raise the camera to eye level

Prop your phone or laptop on books so the camera is roughly level with your eyes. Looking down at a camera on the table gives everyone an unflattering view up your nose — and it's tiring to hunch.

3. Get closer to the microphone

Your device's built-in microphone is fine if you're within arm's reach and the room is quiet. Close doors, turn off the TV, and avoid sitting under a noisy fan. If people say you sound distant, move closer or use earphones with a built-in mic — even basic ones help enormously.

Learn the three buttons that matter

Every video app has the same essential controls, usually at the bottom of the screen:

  • Microphone (mute/unmute): tap to mute yourself when you're not speaking — especially in group calls. Background noise (dogs, traffic, typing) is the most common annoyance for everyone else.
  • Camera (video on/off): tap to turn your video off if you need a moment. Your name or photo will show instead.
  • End call (usually red): the phone-icon button hangs up. Don't just close the app — actually tap this, or you might stay connected.

There's often a chat button too — useful for typing a message if your microphone isn't working ("I can hear you but you can't hear me — let me rejoin").

Fixing the most common problems

  • "They can't hear me": check you're unmuted; check the app has microphone permission in your phone's Settings; if using earphones, unplug them and test the built-in mic.
  • "They can't see me": check the camera isn't covered (a sticker or case flap); check camera permission in Settings; make sure you didn't tap the video-off button.
  • Freezing or lagging: move closer to your Wi-Fi router, or switch off your video for a while (audio-only calls need far less bandwidth). Ask others in the house to pause streaming during important calls.
  • Echo: usually caused by two devices in the same room both on the call, or speakers too loud. Use earphones — it almost always fixes echo instantly.
  • "The link doesn't work": try copying the link into a different browser, or update the app. If all else fails, ask the host to re-send the invitation.

Good video call manners

  • Join a couple of minutes early for important calls.
  • Mute yourself when you're not speaking in group calls.
  • Look at the camera (not the screen) when you're talking — it feels like eye contact to the other person.
  • Avoid multitasking visibly; people can tell.
  • If your connection is bad, say so early rather than suffering silently — most people are understanding.

Frequently asked questions

Do video calls cost money?

The apps listed here are free for personal use, and calls over Wi-Fi don't use your mobile plan minutes. On mobile data (not Wi-Fi), video calls do use data — roughly half a gigabyte per hour — so prefer Wi-Fi for long calls.

Can I do a video call on my phone, or do I need a computer?

Your phone is perfectly fine — most people video-call from their phones. A computer gives you a bigger screen, which helps for group meetings, but it's not required.

Is it safe to click a video call link from someone I don't know?

Joining a call via a link is generally safe — the risk is in downloading things. Never install software just because a caller told you to, and never give remote control of your device to an unexpected caller claiming to be "tech support."

What if I'm nervous about being on camera?

That's completely normal. Remember: everyone else is focused on themselves, not you. Start with audio-friendly practice calls with family, keep your first calls short, and know that you can always turn your camera off if you need a break.
