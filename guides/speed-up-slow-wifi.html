How to Speed Up Slow Wi-Fi at Home: 9 Proven Fixes

By the Everyday Tech Help editorial team · Published September 27, 2026

Video calls freeze, pages crawl, and streaming turns into a slideshow of buffering wheels. Before you blame your internet provider or buy a new router, know this: most slow Wi-Fi problems are caused by things inside your home, and most of them are free to fix. Work through these nine fixes in order — they go from the quickest wins to the deeper solutions.

First: check what "slow" actually means

Run a quick speed test before changing anything, so you know whether your fixes are working. On your phone or computer, open your browser and search for "speed test," then use the built-in test that appears. Write down the number. Test again after each fix. You're looking for the download speed number, measured in Mbps (megabits per second).

As a rough guide for what you actually need:

What you're doingSpeed you need
Browsing and email5–10 Mbps
Video calls10–20 Mbps
Streaming HD video15–25 Mbps
Streaming 4K video25–50 Mbps
Online gaming20+ Mbps with low latency

If your test shows far less than what you're paying for, the fixes below are worth your time.

Fix 1: Restart your router (properly)

Yes, everyone suggests this, because it genuinely works. But do it right: unplug the router's power cable, wait a full 30 seconds (count it out — this clears the router's short-term memory), then plug it back in. Give it two to three minutes to fully reconnect before testing. Do the same for your modem if it's a separate box.

Fix 2: Move your router to a better spot

Router placement is the single most underestimated factor in Wi-Fi speed. Wi-Fi signals spread outward and downward from the router, and they struggle through walls, metal, and water.

  • Put it centrally. A router stuffed in a corner of the house serves that corner brilliantly and the far bedroom terribly. The middle of your home, as close as you can manage, is ideal.
  • Raise it up. On a shelf or bookcase, not on the floor behind the sofa. Signals travel better slightly downward than upward through floors.
  • Keep it in the open. Inside a cabinet or behind the TV is one of the worst spots. It needs airflow and a clear path for the signal.
  • Keep it away from microwaves, baby monitors, cordless phone bases, thick concrete walls, mirrors, and large metal objects like filing cabinets or refrigerators.

Fix 3: Point the antennas correctly

If your router has external antennas, position matters. For a single-floor home, point them straight up. For a multi-floor home, point one straight up and the others at a 45-degree angle — this spreads the signal both horizontally and vertically.

Fix 4: Switch to the 5 GHz network

Most modern routers broadcast two networks: a 2.4 GHz one and a 5 GHz one (the names usually look like "HomeWiFi" and "HomeWiFi_5G"). The 5 GHz band is much faster over short distances but doesn't travel as far through walls. The 2.4 GHz band is slower but reaches further.

  • In the same room as the router or one room away: use 5 GHz.
  • Far from the router or through several walls: 2.4 GHz may actually be more reliable.

If your devices keep connecting to the slower band automatically, you can "forget" the 2.4 GHz network on devices you mostly use near the router.

Fix 5: Change the Wi-Fi channel

Think of Wi-Fi channels like lanes on a highway. In apartment buildings, dozens of routers fight over the same lanes, and everything slows down. You can switch your router to a less crowded channel.

  1. On your phone, install a free Wi-Fi analyzer app and look at which channels nearby networks are using.
  2. Log into your router's settings by typing its address into a browser — this is usually 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1 (it's often printed on a sticker on the router itself, along with the admin password).
  3. Find the wireless settings and change the channel to one with the least competition. For the 2.4 GHz band, channels 1, 6, and 11 are the classic good choices because they don't overlap each other.
  4. Save, let the router restart, and test your speed again.

Fix 6: Update your router's firmware

Router manufacturers release software updates that fix bugs and improve performance, but routers almost never update themselves. In your router's settings page (the same 192.168.1.1 page from Fix 5), look for a "Firmware Update" or "Software Update" section and check for updates. This takes a few minutes and the router will restart.

Fix 7: Kick off bandwidth hogs and freeloaders

Your Wi-Fi is shared among everything connected to it. Common culprits:

  • Cloud backups and large uploads running in the background (check your computer for backup software syncing).
  • Smart TVs and streaming boxes that auto-play previews.
  • Old devices you forgot about — that ancient tablet in a drawer may still be connected.
  • Neighbors on your network. If you never changed the default Wi-Fi password, check the connected-devices list in your router settings for anything you don't recognize, and change the password.

Fix 8: Use a wired connection for stationary devices

Anything that doesn't move — a desktop computer, smart TV, or game console — will be faster and more stable on a wired Ethernet connection. This also frees up Wi-Fi capacity for your phones and laptops. Ethernet cables are inexpensive, and most routers have spare ports on the back.

Fix 9: Consider a mesh system or extender (for big homes)

If your home is large, has thick walls, or multiple floors, a single router may simply not cover it. A mesh Wi-Fi system (two or three units that work together as one network) is the most effective upgrade — far better than old-style range extenders, which halve your speed. Place the extra units halfway between the router and the dead zones.

When to call your internet provider

If you've tried the fixes above and speeds are still far below what you're paying for — especially if a wired connection directly to the modem is also slow — the problem is likely on your provider's side. Call them with your speed test results in hand. Ask them to check the line quality and whether your plan's speeds are actually reaching your home.

Frequently asked questions

Will a new router fix my slow Wi-Fi?

Sometimes, but only if your current router is very old (5+ years) or can't handle your speed plan. Try the free fixes first — a new router in a bad location will still give you bad Wi-Fi.

Why is my Wi-Fi slow in only one room?

That's almost always a distance or obstruction problem. Something between the router and that room — thick walls, a mirror, a metal appliance — is blocking the signal. Try Fix 2 and Fix 9.

Does having many devices slow down Wi-Fi?

Yes, especially older devices. Every connected device shares the router's attention. Disconnecting gadgets you don't use can make a noticeable difference.

My speed test is fine but video calls still freeze. Why?

Speed tests measure download speed, but video calls need stable upload speed and low latency too. Look at the upload number and ping in your test results — if upload is very low, that's your culprit, and Fixes 4 and 8 help most.
